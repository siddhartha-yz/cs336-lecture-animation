Start right here, with the probabilities a model assigns to the actual tokens as it reads from left to right. Multiply those probabilities and the product shrinks with length. Eventually, ordinary floating-point arithmetic can even round it to zero. So take the negative log probability of each token instead. Average those losses across the test set, then exponentiate. That number is perplexity. There is a neat way to picture it: an effective number of choices. With a vocabulary of a hundred thousand tokens, uniform guessing gives you a perplexity of a hundred thousand. Bring it down to ten and you pay the same average loss as a model guessing uniformly among ten options at each step. The actual number of plausible options can change from word to word.

Before comparing two models on perplexity, watch what happens to the exact same sentence under two tokenizers. The words stay put; only the cuts move. Hold the total surprise fixed and split the passage into more pieces. The per-token average falls, even though the score for the text as a whole hasn't changed. Count bits, divide by the raw UTF-eight bytes, and you have bits per byte. Same text, same denominator. The models can still give it different probabilities. For a long time, language modeling benchmarks asked you to train and test on splits of the same corpus, like Penn Treebank or WikiText one oh three. GPT-two took a different route: pretrain on broad web text, then measure zero-shot perplexity on those benchmark corpora, without fitting a model to each one.

If perplexity sits at the heart of pretraining, is driving it down enough to capture everything we mean by capability? Imagine for a moment that your model knew the true joint distribution of questions and answers. Conditioning on a question would give you the right probabilities for its answers. With a rule for what counts as a good decision, you could act on those probabilities. There is a big distance between that ideal and the models we can actually build. Perplexity puts the same weight on every target token. A predictable bit of grammar takes up exactly as much room in the average as the answer does. Look at this one arithmetic sequence. One model saves enough loss on the earlier tokens to win on the whole line. But at the answer slot it gives the wrong choice its highest probability. The other model loses on the average and gets that one decision right.

A global average can hide trouble across domains, too. A model can do well on one kind of writing and turn out to be much less sure somewhere else. Paloma keeps those slices visible. Give each domain its own column and you can see where the probability assignments hold up, and where they don't. A pooled score puts that detail back into one number. There is another problem when the only thing a leaderboard receives is a score. The bars are supposed to be probabilities, and all their mass together has to be exactly one. A claimed score gives you no way to check that. So run the forward pass in a controlled sandbox, apply softmax to the logits, and check the total probability mass. Then compute the loss yourself. Checking the sum is a useful guard, but the score still has to come from the actual model.

Even with the units aligned, the domains split out, and the probabilities verified, perplexity still grades the whole stream of text. A hard question often has just one spot where the answer matters. Standardized exams put that spot at the center: a known answer, automatic grading, and questions across dozens of subjects. That gives us a different ruler. For a while, the ruler gave us clear steps upward. But as the strongest models crowded toward the top, the gaps became harder to read. That's benchmark saturation.
